2. Your combinations

How the recommendations are built

Step 1 — ZIP code to ecological zone

A ZIP code is a postal object, not an ecological one, so it is resolved in two passes. The ZIP is first matched against the published USPS state ranges. A table of 880 three-digit prefix overrides then splits states that straddle real ecological boundaries — the Cascade crest, the Colorado Front Range, the Trans-Pecos, the Florida peninsula, the Sierra Nevada, the Appalachian highlands — and narrows the plausible hardiness span. You confirm both before anything is recommended. The ten regions map onto EPA Level I and Level II ecoregions (the Omernik framework) rather than onto state lines.

For true ZIP-level precision, run fetch_authoritative.py. With network access it downloads the Census ZCTA centroids, the EPA Level III/IV ecoregion polygons and the 2023 USDA hardiness raster, intersects them, and writes an exact per-ZIP lookup that this page picks up automatically.

Step 2 — filtering the species pool

A species is kept only if it is native to the chosen ecoregion, its hardiness range covers your zone, it tolerates your light level and it tolerates your soil moisture. Height limit, deer pressure and the spreading-plants switch trim it further. Cold hardiness is treated as a hard limit; the warm end is treated as a soft limit with one zone of tolerance, because published upper zones are a rough proxy for heat tolerance rather than a measurement. Any plant recommended above its published limit is flagged in that bed's notes.

Step 3 — layered design templates

Each combination comes from a design template built on the layered plant-community model set out by Thomas Rainer and Claudia West in Planting in a Post-Wild World. Every bed gets four functional layers, which is the difference between a designed bed and a pile of plants:

  • Structural (roughly 10–25% of area) — the tall, legible forms that hold the composition together in every season.
  • Seasonal theme (25–40%) — the flowering display, sequenced so something is always coming into bloom.
  • Groundcover matrix (up to 55%) — a continuous living carpet of sedges, grasses and low perennials. This is the layer amateur beds omit, and it is what suppresses weeds and replaces annual mulching.
  • Dynamic filler (2–10%) — short-lived self-sowers that knit a young planting together while the rest establishes.

Within a layer, species are chosen by a weighted draw that favours high ecological value, never repeats a genus, honours the template's colour discipline and, where the template demands it, guarantees continuous bloom or a minimum number of host plants. A usage penalty across the whole result set stops the twelve designs converging on the same six plants. Quantities come from each layer's share of your bed area divided by the species' spacing, assuming triangular spacing, and nudged to odd numbers because odd-numbered drifts read better.

What the habitat scorecard measures

Caterpillar capacityLepidoptera species supported by the genera in the bed, from the National Wildlife Federation and Tallamy host dataset, summed per genus so three asters do not count three times. Caterpillars are the primary food of most North American terrestrial birds — a single chickadee brood needs somewhere between 6,000 and 9,000 of them.
Specialist beesSpecies documented by Fowler and Droege as pollen hosts for oligolectic native bees, which cannot substitute another plant.
Bloom continuityMonths with at least one species in flower, plus the count of months with three or more — the measure that matters for sustaining a pollinator community rather than merely attracting visitors.
Ground coverageShare of the bed held by the living groundcover layer. Above roughly 50% you can stop mulching.
Winter habitatSpecies with standing stems or persistent seedheads, which shelter overwintering bees and moth pupae and feed finches and juncos.
Deliberate omissions. No double-flowered cultivars, which are often sterile or nectarless; no dark-foliage nativars where anthocyanin load is associated with reduced caterpillar survival; and no species listed as invasive anywhere in the range it is recommended for. Where a named cultivar genuinely outperforms the species — sturdier stems, better mildew resistance — it is named in the plant notes instead.

Plant database

Data sources

Every figure in this tool traces to one of the following. Nomenclature follows USDA PLANTS cross-checked against Plants of the World Online; native ranges follow BONAP and USDA PLANTS; ecological attributes follow the NWF and Tallamy host dataset, the Fowler and Droege specialist-bee compilations and the Xerces Society regional lists; cultural requirements follow the Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center.

Before you plant

  1. Check nativity at the county level, not the state level. This tool works at ecoregion resolution. Look each species up on BONAP's North American Plant Atlas and confirm your county is shaded. A plant native 300 miles away is still an introduction.
  2. Buy from a genuine native nursery and ask about neonicotinoids. Systemic insecticides applied in production persist in nectar and pollen for a season or more and will poison exactly the insects the bed is meant to support. Audubon's native plants directory lists local suppliers by ZIP code.
  3. Prefer local ecotypes and seed-grown plants. Local genotypes are adapted to your photoperiod and frost dates and preserve regional genetic diversity. Avoid single clones mass-propagated from one parent.
  4. Do not amend, and do not use rich mulch on lean-soil designs. Most of the drought-adapted species here fail in improved soil. Gravel gardens and hellstrips want lean mineral ground.
  5. Plant at the quoted density, or closer. Sparse planting with wide mulched gaps is the single most common failure: weeds fill whatever you leave empty. Aim for full ground coverage within two seasons.
  6. Leave the bed standing over winter and cut back late. Stems and seedheads are habitat, not mess. Cut in two passes in spring, a fortnight apart.
  7. Check local rules. Some municipalities have height or setback ordinances and some homeowners' associations restrict meadow-style planting. A crisp edge, a mown border strip and a small sign do a great deal of political work.
Accuracy notice. Lepidoptera host counts are approximate values at the level of the plant genus and vary substantially by county; treat them as a way to rank plants against each other, not as a measurement. Hardiness zones, native ranges and specialist-bee associations should be verified against the primary sources above for any species you are about to buy in quantity. Cold hardiness is a necessary but not sufficient condition — heat, humidity, soil pH and summer rainfall pattern all matter as much.
